# 3. Organic Intelligence Operating Model

This model turns the philosophy into a repeatable practice for people, teams, products, and communities.

## 3.1 The seven-layer model

1. **Sensing:** What is happening now? What can be directly observed?
2. **Embodiment:** Who is experiencing the situation, and what constraints do bodies and resources create?
3. **Memory:** What has happened before? Which histories and patterns matter?
4. **Meaning:** What does this situation mean to different people and communities?
5. **Deliberation:** Which values and trade-offs should guide the response?
6. **Action:** What is the smallest responsible action that can be tried?
7. **Integration:** What was learned, repaired, shared, or changed in the system?

## 3.2 Distributed intelligence

An OI system should not assume that one central node knows everything. It should allow local knowledge to travel, disagreement to remain visible, and decisions to be made close to the people who experience the consequences.

Design questions:

- What knowledge exists locally but is invisible to the central system?
- Can a person or community explain why a decision is wrong?
- Does the system learn from exceptions rather than erase them?
- What happens when the network is disconnected?
- How does the system recover after failure?

## 3.4 Maturity levels

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 — Metaphor | A useful nature-inspired image, not yet a testable claim. |
| 1 — Observation | A documented biological or social pattern. |
| 2 — Principle | A general design rule derived from observations. |
| 3 — Prototype | A small system or practice that applies the principle. |
| 4 — Evidence | Results are measured across contexts and limitations are reported. |
| 5 — Responsible scale | The practice is governed, auditable, maintainable, and ecologically acceptable. |
